\begin{abstract}
Generated human motion is judged by learned metrics: a neural network reads the motion and returns a score. Such a score should depend on what the body does, not on how the numbers describing it are written. We show that it does not. Joint rotations are usually stored as six numbers, of which only three matter; the other three can be changed without altering the movement at all. Changing only those three, with every joint position and rotation untouched, moves the standard text-to-motion metrics a great deal. Real motion rewritten this way gets a Fr\'echet distance (FID) up to eight times larger than a published generator's, and an attacker with access to the metric can raise caption-retrieval accuracy from 46\% to over 99\%. Smaller effects appear in the released outputs of ten generator settings, and the same trick more than doubles a sign-language translation score. Converting rotations to a canonical form before scoring removes the problem exactly, and we release this fix as a short drop-in function.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A text-to-motion model turns a sentence such as ``a person walks forward and waves'' into a 3D human movement. Judging such models by eye does not scale, so the field relies on learned metrics. A neural \emph{evaluator} turns each motion and each sentence into a vector, so that matching pairs end up close together~\cite{guo2022,tmr2023}, and two numbers are computed from these vectors. \emph{R-Precision} asks whether a motion's true caption is the closest of 32 candidates, its own and 31 from other test motions, so that chance is 1 in 32; the group size of 32 is fixed by the standard protocol (higher is better). The \emph{Fr\'echet distance} (FID)~\cite{heusel2017}, borrowed from image generation, measures how different the vectors of generated motion are, as a group, from those of real motion (lower is better). Both are usually computed on HumanML3D~\cite{guo2022}, the standard collection of human motions paired with written descriptions. These scores rank methods and, increasingly, serve as the reward when a generator is fine-tuned~\cite{tan2026,motionrft2026}.

All of this rests on an assumption so basic that it is rarely stated: writing the same movement in two ways must give the same score.

The assumption is false. \Cref{fig:teaser} shows one generated motion written in two ways: the numbers the generator produced, and a copy in which we changed only the three numbers per joint that the rotation conversion ignores. Every joint position and every joint rotation is identical, and a renderer draws the same body. Yet, when an attacker chooses the rewriting using the evaluator's gradients, the standard HumanML3D evaluator ranks the true caption first for 99.7\% of the rewritten motions and 45.7\% of the originals. Even without an attacker, canonicalizing the released outputs of public generators changes their FID by up to 0.046, more than the 0.022 that separates the published scores of MDM, MLD and MotionLCM.

Where does this freedom come from? Joint rotations are commonly stored in the 6D representation~\cite{zhou2019}: two 3D vectors, from which the rotation is recovered by normalizing the first and removing from the second its component along the first (\cref{fig:teaser}b). This throws information away on purpose. The length of each vector, and how far the second leans toward the first, never affect the result. That leaves three numbers per joint and frame that mean nothing physically. Animation tools and renderers ignore them. A neural evaluator that reads all six numbers has no reason to ignore them, and we find that it does not.

Image evaluation learned a similar lesson earlier. The same images receive different FIDs depending on the resizing filter or compression used~\cite{parmar2022}, and FID can be lowered without improving the images by matching classifier statistics~\cite{kynkaanniemi2023}. These findings changed how image metrics are computed and reported. Motion metrics have had no comparable audit of their input. The closest work, MARDM~\cite{mardm2025}, observed that the HumanML3D evaluator leans on feature groups that generators do not need, and proposed evaluators that ignore them. Its perturbations, however, also change the movement. We ask a stricter question: can the \emph{same} rotations, written differently, receive different scores? Answering it takes changes that alter nothing the motion represents, and a remedy that keeps the rotations, since some data, such as the sign-language features studied here, consist of nothing else. That a network reacts to inputs it never saw vary is not surprising in itself. What was unknown is how much this matters in the metrics people actually report, and whether published numbers are affected.

We study this from settings with no access to the evaluator up to an attacker with full access to its gradients:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No access.} Rewriting real motion with one fixed rule, without any caption or gradient, raises its FID to up to eight times that of a published generator, as much as rotating every joint by 10--30$^\circ$ about a fixed axis.
\item \textbf{Released outputs.} Canonicalizing the unmodified outputs of ten public generator settings changes their FID. In six, the change exceeds the margin of error their authors report.
\item \textbf{Gradient access.} Choosing the rewriting with the evaluator's gradients drives retrieval to 99--100\% in two different evaluators, and lowers FID by up to 59\% without using any caption. An attacker who sees only the score, with no gradients, still lowers FID by up to 57\%.
\item \textbf{Sign language.} The same trick more than doubles the backtranslation score of generated sign-language motion, from 6.39 to 15.21 BLEU, and reproduces on real motion in two more corpora.
\end{itemize}
In every case the movement is verified to be unchanged, so a score change cannot mean better or worse motion.

The remedy is simple. Before scoring, replace each pair of vectors by its canonical form, the unit-length perpendicular pair that gives the same rotation. All equivalent writings then become the same input. Unlike discarding rotations~\cite{mardm2025}, this keeps the information, needs no retraining and works for rotation-only data.

\noindent Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item an audit that isolates how much learned motion evaluators depend on the way rotations are written, with controls showing that a change that moves no joint is scored like a 10--30$^\circ$ rotation;
\item evidence that this dependence is present in public generator outputs and can be exploited without changing the motion, in two evaluators, two motion datasets and three sign-language corpora\new{, and that reward fine-tuning can find it without an attacker};
\item a canonical evaluation interface, with a portable implementation, that removes the dependence while keeping all rotational information.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Pitfalls of generative evaluation.}
Learned metrics are imperfect stand-ins for human judgment~\cite{jayasumana2024,stein2023,voas2023}, and implementation details can move them as much as model changes do~\cite{parmar2022,kynkaanniemi2023}. We find a failure of the same kind in motion evaluation. Because we keep the movement fixed exactly, it does not depend on how well a metric agrees with people.

\paragraph{Motion representations and evaluators.}
The 6D rotation representation~\cite{zhou2019} and related projections~\cite{levinson2020} are standard in motion learning. The HumanML3D and KIT-ML features~\cite{guo2022,kit2016} combine root motion, joint positions, velocities, 6D rotations and foot contacts, and are read by the T2M evaluator~\cite{guo2022} and TMR~\cite{tmr2023}. Critics trained on human preferences~\cite{wang2025motioncritic} and reward fine-tuning of generators~\cite{draft2024,tan2026,motionrft2026} make the consistency of such evaluators matter for training as well as reporting. MARDM~\cite{mardm2025} perturbs or removes groups of redundant features and trains evaluators on root motion and positions only. Its perturbations change represented rotations; ours do not, and our remedy keeps them.

\paragraph{Adversarial evaluation.}
Neural predictions can be redirected by universal or learned perturbations~\cite{uap2017,gap2018,atn2018}, including in sign recognizers, image-quality models, translation metrics and text-to-motion systems~\cite{li2023,liu2024,huang2023,alertmotion2025}. Our changes need not be small: they are invisible to the movement by construction. This allows an exact defense, whereas detectors can be evaded by adaptive attacks~\cite{tramer2020adaptive}.

\paragraph{Sign-language production.}
Sign-language generators are trained on German (PHOENIX-2014T~\cite{camgoz2018}), American (How2Sign~\cite{duarte2021}) and Chinese (CSL-Daily~\cite{zhou2021}) corpora. A common automatic score is \emph{backtranslation}: a sign-to-text reader decodes the generated signing, and the text is compared with the reference sentence~\cite{saunders2020,camgoz2020,walsh2025,low2026}. Recent work adds semantic and linguistic checks~\cite{imai2025,cory2026}. We study a requirement on the reader's input, in features that contain only rotations.

\section{Writing the same rotation in many ways}
\subsection{The freedom hidden in 6D rotations}
Store one joint's rotation as two vectors $a,b\in\R^3$ with $a\ne0$ and $b$ not parallel to $a$. The standard conversion~\cite{zhou2019} keeps the direction of $a$ and the part of $b$ perpendicular to it:
\begin{align}
u&=a/\|a\|,\qquad b_\perp=b-(u^\top b)\,u,\nonumber\\
v&=b_\perp/\|b_\perp\|,\qquad R=[u;\,v;\,u\times v].\label{eq:rotation}
\end{align}
Now pick any positive $\alpha,\beta$ and any real $\eta$, and rewrite the pair as
\begin{equation}
\G_{\alpha,\beta,\eta}(a,b)=(\alpha a,\;\beta b+\eta a).\label{eq:transform}
\end{equation}
The first vector still points the same way, and removing its component from the second leaves $\beta b_\perp$, which normalizes to the same $v$. So the rotation $R$ is unchanged. We call this a \emph{recoding}, and $\C(a,b)=(u,v)$ the \emph{canonical form}:
\begin{equation}
R(\G(d))=R(d),\qquad \C(\G(d))=\C(d).\label{eq:invariance}
\end{equation}
Conversely, every way of writing a given rotation is a recoding of its canonical form. Recodings are therefore not a few nearby alternatives: they cover all writings of the rotation, three free numbers per joint and frame.

\begin{figure*}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/motion_consistency_contract.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{What we change and what we leave alone.} A recoding rewrites only the redundant coordinates of the 6D rotations. The converter returns the same rotations, and positions, velocities, contacts and timing are untouched. A raw evaluator sees different inputs for the same movement. Canonicalizing before scoring gives every equivalent writing one input.}\label{fig:contract}\end{figure*}

We apply recodings only to rotation channels (\cref{fig:contract}). Every other channel and all padding is left bit-for-bit identical, and rotations, positions and forward kinematics are checked independently of any score (supplement).

\subsection{Five ways to choose the recoding}
\paragraph{A fixed rule.} The same recoding for every joint and frame: $(\log\alpha,\log\beta,\eta)=(b,-b,b)$ with $b=\pm0.1$ or $\pm0.35$, plus random recodings as a control. No caption, generator or gradient is involved.
\paragraph{Released outputs.} We simply canonicalize each public generator's output and score both versions.
\paragraph{Targeted.} With access to the evaluator's gradients, we optimize the coefficients of each frame and joint, each limited to a range set by a bound $b$, so that the evaluator believes the motion matches a chosen caption: its own, or another clip's.
\paragraph{Caption-blind.} The same optimization with no caption and no test reference. The target is the statistics of the vectors of 2,048 training motions~\cite{bhatia2017}. The results are saved before any reference scoring, and a second official checkpoint, which gave no gradients, scores them as well.
\paragraph{Learned layer.} For sign language, a small network predicts the coefficients from the motion and its source sentence. It is trained once, with the generator and reader frozen, and then needs no optimization per clip~\cite{gap2018,atn2018}.

\subsection{Closing the channel}
Canonicalization removes the freedom. For any deterministic evaluator $f$ and any recoding,
\begin{equation}
(f\circ\C)(\G(x))=(f\circ\C)(x),\label{eq:closure}
\end{equation}
no matter who chose the recoding or how. Detecting unusual inputs is weaker. In the supplement, an attack that keeps both the distance to the canonical form and the feature norm unchanged still adds 4.09 BLEU to a sign-language score, while a threshold detector on that distance performs at chance.

Order matters: canonicalize the raw features first, then normalize them the way the evaluator expects. Canonicalizing numbers that were already normalized does not give the same rotation. The same interface should be used for generated and reference motion. \Cref{alg:canonical} shows the complete interface in eight lines.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textbf{Canonical evaluator input}, NumPy-like pseudocode. \texttt{norm}, \texttt{dot} and \texttt{cat} act on the last axis. Apply it to generated and reference motion and mask padded frames as usual; the released implementation adds input validation.}\label{alg:canonical}
\begin{lstlisting}
# raw: [N, T, D] unnormalized motion features
# mean, std: the evaluator's own statistics
# rot: rotation channels (67:193 in HumanML3D)
def canonical_input(raw, mean, std, rot):
    x = raw.copy()
    d = x[..., rot].reshape(*x.shape[:-1], -1, 6)
    a, b = d[..., :3], d[..., 3:]
    u = a / norm(a)            # direction of a
    v = b - dot(u, b) * u      # remove the a-component
    v = v / norm(v)
    x[..., rot] = cat([u, v]).reshape(*x.shape[:-1], -1)
    return (x - mean) / std    # normalize last
\end{lstlisting}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental setup}
\paragraph{Data and evaluators.}
HumanML3D~\cite{guo2022} and KIT-ML~\cite{kit2016} are the two standard collections of 3D human motion with text descriptions. Their per-frame feature vectors (263 values for HumanML3D, 251 for KIT-ML) hold root motion, joint positions, velocities, foot contacts and the 6D rotations of 21 or 20 joints. We test two evaluators. The \emph{T2M evaluator}~\cite{guo2022} is the standard one for HumanML3D and reads every channel except foot contacts. Two official checkpoints of it are in use. The one released with T2M-GPT~\cite{t2mgpt2023} reproduces the published real-motion scores (top-1 R-Precision 0.517 against the published 0.511), so we use it for the targeted attacks. The fixed rewritings, the audits of released outputs and the caption-blind and query-only attacks use the checkpoint distributed with MDM~\cite{tevet2023} (real-motion R-Precision 0.462), with the other as a replication. \emph{TMR}~\cite{tmr2023} is a Transformer with a different architecture and its own normalization, and scores by cosine similarity. For KIT-ML we use its native evaluator.

\paragraph{Generators.}
We audit the released MDM~\cite{tevet2023}, MLD~\cite{mld2023}, MotionLCM~\cite{motionlcm2024}, T2M-GPT~\cite{t2mgpt2023}, T2M~\cite{guo2022}, MoMask~\cite{momask2024}, DiP, the diffusion planner of CLoSD~\cite{closd2025} (which sees a 20-frame prefix) and MotionDiffuse~\cite{motiondiffuse2024} on HumanML3D, and MoMask and MotionDiffuse on KIT-ML, using their native inference settings. Each setting uses 20 draws of the standard test protocol. Raw and canonical scores share outputs, captions and references, and intervals are paired and adjusted for multiple comparisons. These are consistency audits, not reproductions of published leaderboards.

\paragraph{Attacks.}
Targeted attacks use the same 1,024 test clips for both evaluators, 80 optimization steps and bound $b=0.35$. The bound $b$ limits how far each coefficient may move. Caption-blind attacks use $b=0.1$ (and $0.35$, in the supplement) on 960 MDM outputs for each of three generation seeds, and on MLD. For equal-size comparisons we rescale every change to the same distance $\kappa\sqrt{L}$ in the evaluator's normalized input space, where $L$ is the number of frames.

\paragraph{Sign language.}
We use the released 240-value body and hand rotation features and the public SignSparK backtranslation readers~\cite{low2026,signsparkbt}, one Sign Language Transformer~\cite{camgoz2020} per corpus, on the full test sets of PHOENIX-2014T (642 clips), How2Sign (2,183) and CSL-Daily (1,162). The score is BLEU-4~\cite{papineni2002,post2018}, which counts matching word sequences between decoded text and reference (higher is better; word-level, and character-level for CSL-Daily). PHOENIX layers rewrite the output of a frozen text-to-motion model; How2Sign and CSL-Daily layers rewrite real motion. Each layer is trained with three seeds. Code, configurations and saved outputs accompany the submission.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Rewriting alone changes the score}
\textbf{A fixed rule is enough.} We rewrite real HumanML3D motion with one fixed rule, with no caption and no optimization. Real motion scored against itself has FID 0.001. After rewriting it scores 2.00 or 3.64, depending on the sign of the rule. The released MDM generator scores 0.455 under the same protocol, so the rewritten real motion looks four to eight times worse than a generator, although nothing about it has moved. Smaller rewritings and random ones change FID by only 0.03 to 0.08. \Cref{fig:scale} (left) shows the same pattern against a paired reference (1.97 and 3.57).

\textbf{The change is as large as a visible one.} To put this on a physical scale we also rotate the joints for real by known angles (\cref{fig:scale}, right). The two largest rewritings score like a rotation of 1--3$^\circ$ applied independently to each frame, or of 10--30$^\circ$ about a fixed axis. A rewriting that moves nothing is scored like several degrees of real movement.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/within_rotation_budget.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Equal size, unequal response.} Change in FID of real motion for changes of equal normalized size inside the 126 rotation channels. Redundant directions (orange) leave every rotation unchanged; genuine directions (blue) rotate the joints. Means over 20 draws; intervals are narrower than the bars.}\label{fig:within}\end{figure}

\textbf{Redundant directions count more than real ones.} \Cref{fig:within} compares changes of the same size within the 126 rotation channels. A fixed redundant direction moves FID by 0.08 to 0.12. A fixed direction of the same size that really rotates the joints moves it by 0.01 to 0.015, about 7.7 times less. The evaluator reacts more to numbers that carry no movement than to numbers that do. The comparison is specific to these fixed directions; random redundant changes are weaker than random real rotations.

\textbf{A possible reason.} Real HumanML3D rotations are already canonical, so the evaluator never saw the redundant numbers vary during training and was never taught to ignore them. A fixed rewriting shifts every frame the same way, which may be why it matters more than random noise. Our controls establish the difference, not this explanation.

\subsection{Released generators are affected too}
Generators do not output perfectly canonical rotations, so the dependence is already inside their reported scores. We canonicalize each released output, score it again, and change nothing else (\cref{tab:natural}). FID changes for all ten settings, by up to 0.046, with both signs, even among diffusion models. The published FIDs in \cref{tab:natural} are for scale only, since protocols differ. In six of ten settings, the change is larger than the margin of error the authors report for their own FID. For scale, the published FIDs of MDM, MLD and MotionLCM are 0.489, 0.473 and 0.467, a spread of 0.022, which is smaller than the encoding-only change of MotionLCM (0.032) or T2M-GPT (0.034). An improvement of that size could therefore come from the encoding alone. With the main evaluator, the FID ranking of all eight HumanML3D releases is identical before and after canonicalization (Kendall $\tau=1$). With the second checkpoint, MotionLCM and MLD swap places in the means: MotionLCM is ahead by 0.001 FID on raw features, and MLD by 0.008 after canonicalization. Canonicalization changes the gap between them reliably (by 0.009, 95\% interval [0.005, 0.013]), but under either encoding the two are statistically tied. Across all 28 generator pairs with the main checkpoint and 10 with the second, no ranking flips with intervals that exclude zero on both sides (supplement). Retrieval gaps are small throughout. For released models, then, the effect is a real inconsistency, comparable to small claimed improvements, but it does not overturn any ranking that was statistically supported.

\textbf{Retrieval reaches its ceiling.} With the evaluator's gradients, recoding raises the true caption's top-1 rank from 0.457 to 0.997 for MDM outputs and from 0.517 to 0.998 for real motion in the T2M evaluator, and from 0.578 to 0.993 and 0.688 to 1.000 in TMR (\cref{tab:retrieval}). The MDM-distributed checkpoint behaves the same way (0.397 to 0.993; supplement). The evaluator is not recognizing better movement. When the target is another clip's caption, that wrong caption is retrieved 92\% to 97\% of the time and the true one is lost. If the same attack is run against an evaluator that canonicalizes first, nothing changes: its outputs differ by less than $10^{-5}$.

\textbf{No caption is needed to lower FID.} Using only statistics of training motion as the target, the rewriting lowers the FID of MDM outputs by 39\% to 53\% across three generation seeds, and of MLD outputs by 59\%. A second official checkpoint of the evaluator, which gave no gradients, still rates the same outputs 12\% to 25\% better (supplement). These changes are larger than the gaps between several published generators (\cref{tab:natural}), and no joint has moved.

\textbf{Gradients are not needed either.} We next give the attacker only one number per query: the FID of its rewritten outputs against the training motions, with no gradients, captions or test reference. A simple evolution strategy over 63 per-joint coefficients then lowers the held-out test FID by 11\% to 57\% after 300 queries (bound 0.1, four cohorts), and the gains carry over to the second checkpoint, which was never queried (4\% to 41\%). Random coefficients of the same size raise FID instead. With a larger bound the queried FID falls further, by 29\% to 68\%, but these gains do not carry over (supplement).

\subsection{Sign language, where rotations are all there is}
\input{table_main_sign}
Sign-language features consist only of rotations, so there is no position channel to fall back on. \emph{Backtranslation} scores a generator by letting a sign-to-text reader decode the generated signing and comparing the text with the reference sentence.

\textbf{Real inputs already show the dependence.} Without any attack or caption, canonicalizing released real features changes the decoded sentence for 66\% of How2Sign clips (1,438 of 2,183) and 40\% of CSL-Daily clips (464 of 1,162), and for none on PHOENIX. Random noise of the same size changes fewer (42\% and 22\%), so the readers are more sensitive to this direction than to random noise. A real physical change of the same size changes slightly more (70\% and 44\%), so they are sensitive to physical changes as well. These readers are also weak (3.5 BLEU on How2Sign), so this should be read as a property of these readers.

\textbf{A learned layer can steer the score.} A small layer reads the generated motion and its source sentence and predicts the coefficients. On PHOENIX it raises BLEU from 6.39 to 15.21, close to the 15.00 of real signing, while every canonical hypothesis is unchanged (\cref{tab:sign}). If we change only the sentence given to the layer, the decoded text moves toward that sentence: BLEU against it rises from 0.83 to 9.60. On real How2Sign and CSL-Daily motion the same construction adds 2.64 and 0.54 BLEU, with intervals that exclude zero. Optimizing each clip instead of training a layer exposes a larger channel: on PHOENIX the reader decodes an arbitrary other sentence at 85.19 BLEU (\cref{fig:signexample} and supplement).

\subsection{Canonicalization closes the channel}
\textbf{It works exactly.} Canonicalized inputs give identical evaluator outputs and identical decoded sentences for every targeted, caption-blind and learned rewriting above, as \cref{eq:closure} predicts (numerical checks in the supplement). Evaluators that discard rotations~\cite{mardm2025} are also invariant, but they replace the evaluator and cannot be used on rotation-only data. Canonicalization keeps the evaluator, and its ranking of the eight HumanML3D generators agrees with that of the public essential-feature evaluators (Kendall $\tau=0.857$).

\textbf{We measure no accuracy cost.} Real HumanML3D and KIT-ML rotations are already canonical, so real-motion retrieval is unchanged by construction. Real sign-language features are not. Readers fine-tuned on canonical inputs reach test BLEU within $+0.10$ (How2Sign) and $-0.02$ (CSL-Daily) of matched readers fine-tuned on raw inputs, with intervals that contain zero.

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{A checklist for motion evaluation.} Scores should be defined on the movement, not on its encoding. For any evaluator of rotation features we recommend: (i)~name the converter and its canonical form; (ii)~report each method's raw-minus-canonical score gap, since a nonzero gap means the evaluator reads the encoding; (iii)~score generated and reference motion through the same canonical interface (\cref{alg:canonical}); and (iv)~canonicalize learned rewards before their gradients reach a generator, because the redundant numbers are exactly what a generator can change at no cost to its motion.

\new{\paragraph{Training against the evaluator.} We tested item (iv) in a study whose protocol was fixed before any test inference (supplement): MDM and MLD were fine-tuned with a standard reward method~\cite{xu2023imagereward,draft2024}, using the retrieval loss of an official T2M checkpoint as reward (each of the two for MDM, three seeds each). Rewarded on its raw output, MDM, which writes the feature vector directly, moved off canonical form in all six runs, to more than twice the pretrained level, and 0.8 to 0.9 points of the R-Precision it was trained to raise came from the encoding alone. Runs rewarded on canonicalized output stayed at the pretrained level. With MDM's checkpoint as reward, the same outputs score an FID of 0.75 raw and 0.28 canonicalized, on either side of the pretrained model's 0.33; with the other checkpoint the FID changes are smaller and of either sign. MLD, whose frozen decoder produces the features, moved off canonical form as much under canonical as under raw reward. Canonicalizing the reward removes the incentive to use the redundant numbers, but not every drift; canonicalizing the evaluation removes their effect on the score either way.}

\paragraph{Other rotation formats.}
The audit needs only a converter that maps many writings to one rotation. Unnormalized quaternions are indifferent to positive scale and to sign, and axis--angle vectors to adding a full turn. We have not tested these formats, but the procedure is the same: rewrite within the converter's equivalence class, compare scores, and canonicalize before reading.

\paragraph{Scope.}
Our claims concern evaluation. We do not claim that published results were manipulated, or that standard training exploits this channel\new{; our fine-tuning study covers one reward method and two generators}. Our query-only attacker uses a simple search over 63 coefficients; we did not try stronger black-box strategies. Canonicalization removes the dependence on rotation encoding; it does not make an evaluator accurate, and it does not address redundancy between positions and velocities or attacks that change the movement. The sign-language readers come from one released family, and their accuracy on How2Sign and CSL-Daily is low.

\section{Conclusion}
Learned motion evaluators give different scores to the same movement when its rotations are written differently. The effect is present in released generator outputs, can be as large as a visible physical change, and can steer retrieval, FID and backtranslation without moving a joint. Canonicalizing rotations before scoring removes it exactly and keeps all rotational information. As happened for image metrics, we hope that a precise statement of the evaluation interface becomes part of how motion results are computed and reported.
